I remember sitting on the edge of my bed about eight weeks post-op, staring at a counter full of supplement bottles my team had prescribed. Calcium citrate. B-12. Iron. A bariatric multivitamin the size of my thumbnail. Every single one needed water I couldn't drink around meals. Every single one made me feel like I was negotiating with a stomach that had already quit the conversation.

And the pills were the worst part. Not the food. Not the tracking. The pills.

They sat. They got stuck. I'd take them too close to a meal and regret it. I'd take them too far from a meal and forget. I started skipping days. Then weeks. Then I looked at the half-empty bottles and felt like I was already failing at something I was supposed to do for the rest of my life.

Nobody told me it would feel like that. The surgeon explained the procedure. The dietitian handed me the protocol. But nobody sat with me at 7 AM and admitted that the routine itself — the four bottles, the four times a day, the water timing, the sheer friction of it — was going to be the hardest part.

And nobody warned me about the rest.

About month three, my hair started coming out in handfuls. Not a few strands in the shower — actual clumps. I'd run my fingers through my hair and come away with a nest of it. I stood in front of the mirror one morning and saw my scalp through the thinning at my part, and I cried.

Nobody told me that rapid weight loss pushes your hair follicles into a resting phase all at once, so they shed together months later. Nobody told me it had a name, or that it would eventually stop.

I just thought I was falling apart.

My nails went next. They'd split down the middle, peel in layers, catch on everything. I'd look down at my hands during a meeting and see ten tiny reminders that my body was in upheaval.

And my skin. It felt tight and dry no matter how much lotion I used. Flaky patches on my cheeks, my arms, my legs. I'd scratch and leave white marks. The water restrictions around meals meant I was dehydrated more often than I realized, and my skin was the first place to show it.

Three things nobody named for me

Telogen effluvium
The hair. Rapid weight loss pushes a large share of follicles into their resting phase at once, so they shed together a few months later. It starts around month three — and it usually passes.
Onychoschizia
The nails. A fancy word for nails that can't hold themselves together — splitting down the middle, peeling in layers.
Xerosis
The skin. Dry, tight, flaky. The dermatologist gave me the word months after I'd started scratching white marks into my arms.

So there I was — hair shedding, nails splitting, skin flaking, pills sitting in my stomach like gravel — and the only advice I got was stick with it.

I couldn't stick with it. Not like that.
